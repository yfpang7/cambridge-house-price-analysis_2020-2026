1 Why this report exists

I bought a flat in Cambridge around 2022, and a few years on I want to know three things. Has it lost value? When, and at what price, should I sell it? And if I do sell, what would it take to move into a 2-3 bed freehold semi-detached or detached house in or around Cambridge?

Rather than rely on gut feeling or estate agent chatter, I went to the public record. This report walks through the data, shows what I found, and ends with a plain-language summary of what I plan to do about it. The code is folded by default, so click on any “Code” label if you want to see how a number was produced.

2.1 Schema of the dataset

The data comes from the UK Land Registry Price Paid Data, which records every property sale in England and Wales. Each row is one sale, with details such as the price, the date, the postcode and the type of property. Here is what each column means:

Index Column Name Data Type Description
0 transaction_id String Unique identifier for the transaction (UUID wrapped in curly braces).
1 price Float Transaction price in GBP (e.g., 450000).
2 date_transferred String / Date Date of transfer formatted as YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM.
3 postcode String Property postcode (e.g., CB1 2AB).
4 property_type Categorical D = Detached, S = Semi-Detached, T = Terraced, F = Flats/Maisonettes, O = Other.
5 new_build Categorical Y = Newly built property, N = Established residential building.
6 tenure Categorical F = Freehold, L = Leasehold.
7 paon String Primary Addressable Object Name (typically house number or name).
8 saon String Secondary Addressable Object Name (flat/apartment number).
9 street String Street name.
10 locality String Locality / Sub-district area.
11 town_city String Town or City (e.g., CAMBRIDGE).
12 district String Local authority district (e.g., CAMBRIDGE or SOUTH CAMBRIDGESHIRE).
13 county String County (e.g., CAMBRIDGESHIRE).
14 ppd_category_type Categorical A = Standard Price Paid entry, B = Additional Price Paid entry (non-standard transactions).
15 record_status Categorical A = Addition, C = Change, D = Delete.

3.3 Removing Non-Standard Transactions

Not every sale in the register tells us something useful about the normal market. A repossession or a sale to a company, for example, is not priced like an ordinary home sale. I only want ordinary sales, so I keep the standard ones (ppd_category_type of A) that are new records (record_status of A).

A little context on the two categories:

Category A: Standard Sales

  • Definition: Single residential properties sold for full market value.
  • Timeline: Collected by HM Land Registry from January 1, 1995 onwards.
  • Use Case: Represents the vast majority of normal, open-market home sales.

Category B: Additional Price Paid (APPD)

  • Definition: Non-standard or specialized property transactions.
  • Timeline: Introduced and tracked starting October 14, 2013.
  • Included Transactions:
    • Repossessions and transfers under a power of sale
    • Buy-to-let properties flagged via a mortgage
    • Transfers to corporate or non-private owners
    • Properties classed under the “Other” category

3.4.1 Quarterly average price faceted by feature

An overall average hides a lot, because it blends cheap flats with expensive detached houses. So I split the same trend by property attribute. A small helper function keeps the three plots consistent.

Code 
def quarterly_price_by(feature, title):
    df = (
        standard_sales
        .sort('date_transferred')
        .group_by_dynamic(
            'date_transferred',
            every='1q',
            group_by=['city_interest', feature],
        )
        .agg(pl.col('price').mean())
    )
    return (
        ggplot(df, aes(x='date_transferred', y='price', color='city_interest'))
        + geom_line()
        + facet_wrap(feature, ncol=2, scales='free_y')
        + labs(title=title, x='Quarter', y='Average price', color='City')
    )

4 Flat depreciation vs other property types

The question: how have flat prices moved compared with other property types? The answer should help me decide when, and at what price, to sell the flat I bought around 2022 (four years ago), and what a 2-3 bed freehold semi-detached or detached house would cost in the cities I’m considering.

A few ground rules for the numbers below:

  • I use median prices, which are not thrown off by a handful of very expensive sales.
  • Figures are in nominal GBP, so they are not adjusted for inflation.
  • 2026 is only a partial year (the data ends in August 2026), and recent sales are still being registered. Full-year comparisons therefore stop at 2025.

def change_table(df):
    return (
        df
        .filter(pl.col('year').is_in([2020, 2022, 2023, 2025]))
        .group_by(['property_type', 'year'])

        .pivot(on='year', index='property_type', values='price')

6 Summary and recommendations

Here is what I take away from all this.

Flats vs other types

  1. Flats have appreciated much less than houses since 2020. In Cambridge they have also lost value since 2023 (about -11%, or about -13% from the peak), while houses held their value or rose.
  2. The Cambridge-wide median overstates the fall for resale flats (about +3% since 2022), because fewer expensive new builds are being sold now. The fair comparison is against resale figures for your own postcode district.
  3. Prices have been stable since early 2024, so there is no evidence that waiting will bring a rebound. Volumes are low, so price realistically and be ready to negotiate.

Selling the flat

  • The benchmark is resale flats in your postcode district (see the table above). Roughly flat since 2022 is the base case, though a new-build flat bought in 2022 may sell for less than was paid.
  • Cheaper commuter-belt flats (Stevenage at about £200-207k) are the only ones still creeping upward.
  • Monthly seasonality in the data is weak and noisy, so I wouldn’t time the sale around it.

Buying a freehold 2-3 bed semi/detached

  • Semis cost about £405-465k and have been flat for about two years, so there is no urgency.
  • The best value in resale semis is in CB23/CB24/CB25 around Cambridge (about £375-380k), then SG2 Stevenage (about £385k) and SG8 Royston (about £405k). Saffron Walden is the most expensive of the four.
  • Buy resale, not new build, to avoid the roughly 14% premium.
  • A resale semi costs roughly £145k more than a resale flat in Cambridge, and £205-230k more in Royston, Stevenage and Saffron Walden (see the upgrade table). The flat’s sale proceeds will need topping up, so run the upgrade table against your own budget.

Limitations

None of this is a valuation, and a few caveats are worth keeping in mind:

  • Medians describe typical sales, not any specific home. Size, condition, floor, lease length, service charge and street all matter, and none of them are in this dataset.
  • There is no bedroom count, so 2-3 bed is approximated by property type and price band.
  • Prices are nominal, so inflation makes the real picture weaker than shown.
  • 2026 data is partial and subject to registration lag, so recent quarters (especially 2026 new builds) may be revised.
